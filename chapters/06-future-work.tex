\chapter{Future Work}\label{ch:futurework}

In its current version, \textit{AntCalc} is implemented entirely in \textit{Wolfram 
Mathematica}. This is the right language to build a trial-stage programme of its 
nature due to its forgiving mathematical environment and its general adoption 
throughout the field. But this choice of language comes at a cost: efficiency. 
Though convenient, \textit{Mathematica} is slow and memory-hungry. In its current state,
running the full \texttt{BuildRRatio} call takes three to four hours and, on 
machines with less RAM, may cause the calculation to crash halfway through. It is then 
apparent that the next step in \textit{AntCalc}'s development is taking it mostly out of 
the \textit{Mathematica} environment and making it a truly modular, multi-language programme 
that exploits only the best characteristics of each tool, including \textit{Mathematica},
keeping the current high-level workflow. 
The current plan for this step is to make the orchestrator a \textit{Python} programme 
and to store the profiles in plain, machine-readable files, such as those in the \texttt{JSON} format.
Then, retain \textit{FeynArts} and \textit{FeynCalc} for diagram generation and amplitude 
manipulation, respectively, but hand the algebraic manipulations required to obtain the 
antenna, or its components, over to \textit{FORM}. Then, lastly, make IBP reduction faster and more stable by replacing 
\textit{LiteRed2} with \textit{Kira}, written in \textit{C++}, keeping \textit{Mathematica}
as an algebraic simplifier.

This step will then allow us to keep expanding \textit{AntCalc}'s 
profile library without fear of hitting a computational bottleneck. The first point of 
expansion would be to the rest of the massive quark-antiquark final-final antenna functions,
especially $A_4^0$, $B_4^0$ and $C_4^0$ at first, and then $A_3^1$ and $A_2^2$. 
After these are completed, expanding the 
scope to other relevant antenna types, particularly the quark-gluon ($D$ and $E$) 
and the gluon-gluon ($F$, $G$ and $H$) antennae, both in the massless and in the 
massive regimes becomes the logical next step. Lastly, extensions to kinematics other than final-final, namely 
initial-initial and initial-final configurations, 
and higher perturbative orders, particularly N$^3$LO, antennae would also be relevant. 

Connections to other established tools would also be pertinent. A connection to a 
Feynman diagram generation tool such as \textit{MadGraph} \cite{Madgraph} and one to a parton-level 
event generator
such as \textit{NNLOJet} \cite{Nnlojet} could allow us to generate more complex 
antennae, from more complex topologies and also enable the numerical validation 
of the results.
